0. PURPOSE AND RELATION TO PRIOR AUDITS

Audits #30–#33 examined inverse stability, experimental hypothesis separation, budget-constrained experiment design, and robust design under model uncertainty. Audits #34–#35 examined simplicity-based model selection and its formal limits.

This audit addresses a distinct decision question: when available experiments cannot distinguish candidate models, does that uncertainty necessarily prevent a defensible decision? Conversely, when should an experiment be selected because it can change a decision rather than merely increase a generic information score?

The conclusions below are conditional mathematical results. They do not establish that a particular OMEGA-ISOP physical model, observation map, loss function, or experimental family has already been specified.

1. FORMAL SETUP

Let H be a nonempty set of candidate hypotheses, E a declared set of admissible experiments, A a set of available actions, and D an observation space.

For each hypothesis h in H and experiment e in E, let P_h^e be the probability distribution of observations under that hypothesis and experiment. Let L(a,h) be the loss assigned to taking action a when h is the relevant state of the world.

The observation model, experiment class, action set, and loss function must be specified before a decision-theoretic conclusion can be evaluated. The results in Sections 3–4 use state-based loss L(a,h). If loss depends on the observation, use L(a,d,h) and define the relevant expected loss and posterior risk explicitly; equality of L(a,h) alone is not enough to establish decision equivalence for that observation-dependent objective.

For a fixed, non-adaptive experiment, P_h^e denotes the full observation law for that experiment. For repeated, dependent, or adaptive designs, the model must instead specify the joint law of the full observation history, or equivalently the conditional law of each next observation given the preceding history, the chosen experiment, and h. Claims about indistinguishability of an entire adaptive protocol require equality of these full-history laws, not merely equality of separate marginal distributions.

2. EXACT OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE

For a fixed experiment family with a specified observation law, define h ~_E h' if and only if

P_h^e = P_{h'}^e for every e in E.

This is observational equivalence relative to the declared experiment class E. It is not a claim of metaphysical identity, universal equivalence under all possible experiments, or equivalence under an experiment excluded from E.

For non-adaptive experiments, equality of the stated observation laws means that an observation-based test has the same distribution under h and h'. For a sequential or adaptive protocol, the corresponding conclusion holds only if the full observation-history law is equal under h and h' for every admissible protocol. A sufficient condition is that the conditional distribution of the next observation is identical under h and h' at every reachable history and for every experiment the protocol may choose there.

Any distinction made by a decision rule must therefore arise from prior information, additional assumptions, external evidence, or a different experiment class, rather than from a difference in the declared observation law.

3. DECISION-RELEVANT EQUIVALENCE

Define decision-relevant equivalence by

h ~_L h' if and only if L(a,h) = L(a,h') for every a in A.

Proposition 1 (decision irrelevance under equal loss profiles).
If h ~_L h', uncertainty about which of h and h' is true does not change the loss assigned to any available action. Therefore, changing probability mass between these two hypotheses while holding the remaining model components fixed does not change the expected loss of any action. Resolving which of the two hypotheses is true is unnecessary for choosing an action by this loss function alone, provided the stated action set and loss function are complete for the decision at hand.

Proof.
For every a in A, L(a,h)=L(a,h') by definition of ~_L. Reassigning probability mass between h and h' therefore leaves the contribution of these states to the expected loss of each action unchanged. Hence action-loss comparisons are unchanged by that reassignment. This conclusion does not require observational equivalence.

Boundary.
Observational equivalence is relevant to whether the permitted data can distinguish h and h'; equal loss profiles are relevant to whether the distinction matters for this specified decision objective. Neither condition establishes scientific equivalence in general. The hypotheses may differ in predictions outside E, in unmodeled consequences, or under a different loss function or action set.

4. WHEN OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE MATTERS FOR A DECISION

If h and h' have the same full observation law under every permitted protocol but their loss profiles differ, the permitted observations cannot distinguish the hypotheses through differences in that law, even though the preferred action may depend on which hypothesis is true.

In that case, the decision cannot be justified by claiming that the data selected one hypothesis over the other. A decision can still be made using an explicit rule such as minimax loss, expected loss under stated prior probabilities, a robust constraint, or an external evidence source. These are decision rules under uncertainty, not empirical identification of the true hypothesis.

Proposition 2 (no data-only resolution within an exact equivalence class).
If h and h' induce the same full observation-history law for the permitted protocol, any test based only on those observations has the same distribution of test outcomes under h and h'. In particular, its rejection probability is the same under both hypotheses, so the test cannot empirically identify which one holds from those observations alone.

This proposition does not prohibit a decision. It prohibits presenting that decision as if the permitted observations had discriminated the hypotheses.

5. EXPERIMENT VALUE MUST BE TIED TO THE DECISION TARGET

A generic information score and a decision-value score answer different questions. An experiment can provide information about parameters or hypotheses while leaving the ranking of available actions unchanged. Conversely, an experiment with modest total information can be valuable if it resolves an uncertainty that changes a high-consequence decision.

For a finite or countable hypothesis set H, let pi be an explicitly stated prior on H. Define the pre-experiment Bayes risk

R(pi) = inf over a in A of sum over h in H of pi(h)L(a,h).

For experiment e, let m_e(d) be the prior-predictive distribution of the observation. Wherever the posterior is well-defined, define the expected posterior Bayes risk as

R_e(pi) = E_{d under m_e} [ inf over a in A of sum over h in H pi(h | d,e)L(a,h) ].

The expected value of sample information is

EVSI(e) = R(pi) - R_e(pi).

For a continuous hypothesis or parameter space, replace sums with integrals and state the prior measure, likelihood, measurability, and integrability conditions needed for the risks to exist. More generally, the risk definitions require well-defined prior-predictive and posterior distributions and finite or otherwise well-defined expected losses. Use an attained minimum only when existence of an optimizer is guaranteed; otherwise use an infimum.

Under these conditions and the loss-minimization convention, EVSI(e) is nonnegative when the decision-maker can ignore the observation and retain any action available before the experiment. The expected posterior-optimal risk cannot exceed the risk of following the preselected pre-experiment action after every observation.

If experiment cost C(e) is included, a simple net-value criterion is EVSI(e) - C(e), provided cost and loss are expressed in commensurable units and their timing and treatment are consistent. This expression is a decision criterion, not a universal measure of scientific value.

All of these quantities depend on the prior, likelihoods, action set, loss, experiment cost, and admissible designs. They are not defined by the Frozen Core unless those components are independently specified.

6. FINITE BUDGETS AND STOPPING

Let B be a finite experiment budget and let E_B be the set of experiment sequences admissible under that budget. A stopping rule should be stated against a declared objective, not chosen after observing which result is convenient.

At a given information history, define V_stop as the minimum expected terminal decision risk available by stopping now and choosing an admissible terminal action. Define V_continue as the infimum expected total value among admissible policies that take at least one further experiment before terminating. A continuation policy must include its possible future observations, subsequent experiment choices, terminal action, and all remaining costs. The continuation set must be nonempty for V_continue to be used; if no experiment is feasible, stopping is mandatory.

Use the same state, loss, uncertainty rule, budget constraints, and cost accounting for both values. Express terminal risk and remaining costs in commensurable units, and include only costs that remain to be paid, not sunk costs already incurred.

The decision-based stopping rule is: stop when V_stop <= V_continue. If V_continue is lower than V_stop, at least one admissible continuation has lower expected total value and stopping is not optimal under the specified model. If the two values are equal, stopping is optimal but may not be unique. When the infimum is not attained, interpret the rule in terms of infima and state whether an optimal policy exists.

Equivalently, when both values are well-defined on the same scale, stop when the best attainable incremental reduction in expected terminal decision risk is no greater than the incremental remaining experiment cost. This equivalence requires consistent accounting and must not be used when the cost and loss terms are not commensurable.

If only one-step experiments are evaluated in place of all admissible continuation policies, the rule is myopic and is justified only when conditions of the design problem make one-step evaluation sufficient. Complementary experiments can have positive joint value even when each experiment has nonpositive incremental value when considered alone. For a sequential design, the continuation value should account for future observations, choices, and costs, for example through a Bellman recursion when its assumptions apply.

This is a criterion within a specified decision model, not a universal stopping theorem. It may be invalid if the model set omits relevant hypotheses, the loss omits important consequences, the prior is unjustified, experiment costs are mismeasured, the observation process is misspecified, or the admissible continuation policies are incompletely enumerated.

A separate identification-based stopping rule may be appropriate when the target is a parameter or hypothesis rather than an action: stop only when the declared target is identified to the required tolerance over the feasible set, or explicitly report that the budget ended before that condition was met. Decision adequacy and scientific identification must not be conflated.

7. CONDITIONAL RESULTS

Result A.
Exact equality of the full observation-history laws under the permitted protocol prevents data-only discrimination using that protocol.

Result B.
If all actions have equal losses under two hypotheses, uncertainty about probability mass assigned between those hypotheses is irrelevant to expected-loss comparisons for that specified objective, with all other model components held fixed.

Result C.
If loss profiles differ across observationally equivalent hypotheses, a decision may still be made by an explicit uncertainty-aware rule, but it cannot be described as data-based identification between those hypotheses.

Result D.
Expected value of sample information is decision-relative. Under the stated assumptions, if the experiment does not reduce the minimum expected decision loss, its EVSI is zero, even if it changes beliefs about the hypotheses.

Result E.
A stopping rule based on expected net decision value is conditional on a sufficiently complete model class, valid probabilities or robust uncertainty sets, a complete loss function, a correctly specified observation process, and consistent cost accounting.

8. NON-IMPLICATIONS

- Observational equivalence under E does not imply equivalence under every possible experiment.
- Failure to identify a hypothesis does not automatically make every decision impossible.
- A decision that is defensible under minimax loss or a stated prior does not prove the selected hypothesis true.
- More information does not guarantee a better decision when its acquisition cost or the decision objective is ignored.
- Zero expected value of sample information under one loss does not imply that the observation has no scientific value under every other objective.
- A finite-budget stopping rule does not certify model-class completeness.
- A posterior preference is not, by itself, an empirical discrimination result when the full observation laws are identical.
- Decision-relevant equivalence is relative to the action set and loss function and can fail when either changes.
- Equality of separate marginal observation distributions alone need not imply equality of the joint law for dependent observations.

9. AUDIT CHECKLIST

Before applying this framework, record:
1. The candidate hypothesis set and its known omissions.
2. The observation distributions and, where relevant, the full joint or conditional observation laws.
3. The exact experiment class, adaptive protocol rules, and budget constraints.
4. The available actions and their loss function.
5. Any prior probabilities or robust uncertainty set and their justification.
6. Whether equivalence is exact or approximate and whether it concerns marginal laws or the full protocol.
7. Whether remaining uncertainty changes action rankings or only model beliefs.
8. Experiment costs, sunk versus remaining costs, and how costs are compared with losses.
9. The decision-based or identification-based stopping criterion.
10. The tolerance required for any claimed target identification.
11. The existence and completeness of the admissible continuation-policy set.
12. The consequences of model misspecification and omitted hypotheses.
13. The scope of the conclusion and the external assumptions on which it depends.

10. FINAL AUDIT CONCLUSION

A finite experiment program should distinguish three outcomes: (i) the hypotheses are empirically separated within the declared design; (ii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent but are decision-equivalent for the stated objective; or (iii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent while their loss profiles differ, requiring an explicit uncertainty-aware decision rule or a more discriminating experiment.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors: treating unresolved model identity as if it made every decision impossible, and treating a practical decision as if it proved which model is true.

The proposed revision supplies a conditional decision framework with more explicit conditions for adaptive observation laws, Bayes risk, and stopping comparisons. It does not establish a physical observation model, empirical result, unique optimal experiment, or universally valid stopping rule for OMEGA-ISOP.
