0. PURPOSE AND RELATION TO PRIOR AUDITS

Audits #30–#33 examined inverse stability, experimental hypothesis separation, budget-constrained experiment design, and robust design under model uncertainty. Audits #34–#35 examined simplicity-based model selection and its formal limits.

This audit addresses a distinct decision question: when available experiments cannot distinguish candidate models, does that uncertainty necessarily prevent a defensible decision? Conversely, when should an experiment be selected because it can change a decision rather than merely increase a generic information score?

The conclusions below are conditional mathematical results. They do not establish that a particular OMEGA-ISOP physical model, observation map, loss function, or experimental family has already been specified.

1. FORMAL SETUP

Let H be a nonempty set of candidate hypotheses, E a declared set of admissible experiments, A a set of available actions, and D the observation space.

For each hypothesis h in H and experiment e in E, let P_h^e be the probability distribution of observations under that hypothesis and experiment. Let L(a,h) be the loss assigned to taking action a when h is the relevant state of the world.

The observation model, experiment class, action set, and loss function must be specified before a decision-theoretic conclusion can be evaluated. The results in Sections 3–4 use state-based loss L(a,h). If loss depends on the observation, use L(a,d,h) and define the relevant expected loss and posterior risk explicitly; equality of L(a,h) alone is not enough to establish decision equivalence for that observation-dependent objective.

2. EXACT OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE

Define h ~_E h' if and only if

P_h^e = P_{h'}^e for every e in E.

This is observational equivalence relative to the declared experiment class E. It is not a claim of metaphysical identity, universal equivalence under all possible experiments, or equivalence under an experiment excluded from E.

If h ~_E h', then no procedure using observations generated by experiments in E can distinguish h from h' through differences in their observation distributions alone. Any distinction made by a decision rule must therefore arise from prior information, additional assumptions, external evidence, or a different experiment class.

3. DECISION-RELEVANT EQUIVALENCE

Define decision-relevant equivalence by

h ~_L h' if and only if L(a,h) = L(a,h') for every a in A.

Proposition 1 (decision irrelevance within an observational class).
Suppose h ~_E h' and h ~_L h'. Then uncertainty between h and h' does not change the loss assigned to any available action. Resolving which of the two hypotheses is true is unnecessary for ranking actions by their losses, provided the stated action set and loss function are complete for the decision at hand.

Proof.
For every a in A, the equality L(a,h)=L(a,h') holds by definition of ~_L. Hence every pairwise action-loss comparison is unchanged when h is replaced by h'. The result concerns the specified decision objective only.

Boundary.
This proposition does not say that h and h' are scientifically equivalent in general. They may differ in predictions outside E, in unmodeled consequences, or under a different loss function or action set.

4. WHEN OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE MATTERS FOR A DECISION

If h ~_E h' but their loss profiles differ, available observations cannot distinguish the hypotheses through the declared experiments, even though the preferred action may depend on which hypothesis is true.

In that case, the decision cannot be justified by claiming that the data selected one hypothesis over the other. A decision can still be made using an explicit rule such as minimax loss, expected loss under stated prior probabilities, a robust constraint, or an external evidence source. These are decision rules under uncertainty, not empirical identification of the true hypothesis.

Proposition 2 (no data-only resolution within an exact equivalence class).
If h ~_E h', every observation procedure restricted to E has the same observation law under h and h'. Therefore, a test based only on those observations cannot have different sampling behavior under h and h' that would empirically identify which one holds.

This proposition does not prohibit a decision. It prohibits presenting that decision as if the permitted observations had discriminated the hypotheses.

5. EXPERIMENT VALUE MUST BE TIED TO THE DECISION TARGET

A generic information score and a decision-value score answer different questions. An experiment can provide information about parameters or hypotheses while leaving the ranking of available actions unchanged. Conversely, an experiment with modest total information can be valuable if it resolves an uncertainty that changes a high-consequence decision.

For a fully specified decision problem, one possible criterion is the expected reduction in Bayes risk. Let pi be an explicitly stated prior on H, and define the pre-experiment Bayes risk

R(pi) = min over a in A of sum over h in H of pi(h)L(a,h).

For experiment e, define its expected posterior Bayes risk as

R_e(pi) = E_{d under the prior-predictive distribution for e} [
  min over a in A of sum over h in H pi(h | d,e)L(a,h)
].

The expected value of sample information is

EVSI(e) = R(pi) - R_e(pi).

Under this loss-minimization convention, EVSI(e) is nonnegative for a well-defined decision problem when the decision-maker can ignore the observation and retain the original action. If experiment cost C(e) is included, a simple net-value criterion is EVSI(e) - C(e), provided the units and timing of cost and loss are made commensurable.

All of these quantities depend on the prior, likelihoods, action set, loss, experiment cost, and admissible designs. They are not defined by the Frozen Core unless those components are independently specified.

6. FINITE BUDGETS AND STOPPING

Let B be a finite experiment budget and let E_B be the set of experiment sequences admissible under that budget. A stopping rule should be stated against a declared objective, not chosen after observing which result is convenient.

A conditional decision-based stopping rule must compare the best remaining admissible continuation with stopping now, not merely evaluate each experiment in isolation.

For the current information state, define the value of a continuation policy as its expected terminal decision risk plus its remaining experiment costs, with all terms expressed in commensurable units. Stop when the minimum expected total value over all admissible continuation sequences or adaptive policies is no lower than the value of stopping now and choosing the best available action. Equivalently, stop when the maximum incremental expected reduction in decision risk minus incremental experiment costs is nonpositive, under the specified model set, prior or robust uncertainty rule, loss function, budget, and cost accounting.

If only one-step experiments are evaluated, the rule is myopic and is justified only when the design problem has conditions that make one-step evaluation sufficient. Complementary experiments can have positive joint value even when each experiment has nonpositive incremental value when considered alone. For a sequential design, the continuation value should account for future observations, choices, and costs, for example through a Bellman recursion when its assumptions apply.

This is a criterion within a specified decision model, not a universal stopping theorem. It may be invalid if the model set omits relevant hypotheses, the loss omits important consequences, the prior is unjustified, experiment costs are mismeasured, or the available continuation policies are incompletely enumerated.

A separate identification-based stopping rule may be appropriate when the target is a parameter or hypothesis rather than an action: stop only when the declared target is identified to the required tolerance over the feasible set, or explicitly report that the budget ended before that condition was met. Decision adequacy and scientific identification must not be conflated.

7. CONDITIONAL RESULTS

Result A.
Exact observational equivalence relative to E prevents data-only discrimination using experiments in E.

Result B.
If all actions have equal losses under two observationally equivalent hypotheses, uncertainty between those hypotheses is irrelevant to the ranking of those actions for that specified loss.

Result C.
If losses differ across observationally equivalent hypotheses, a decision may still be made by an explicit uncertainty-aware rule, but it cannot be described as data-based identification between those hypotheses.

Result D.
Expected value of sample information is decision-relative. Information that does not improve the best achievable expected decision loss may have zero decision value under the declared objective, even if it changes beliefs about the hypotheses.

Result E.
A stopping rule based on expected net decision value is conditional on the completeness of the model class, validity of probabilities or robust uncertainty sets, completeness of the loss, and consistent cost accounting.

8. NON-IMPLICATIONS

- Observational equivalence under E does not imply equivalence under every possible experiment.
- Failure to identify a hypothesis does not automatically make every decision impossible.
- A decision that is defensible under minimax loss or a stated prior does not prove the selected hypothesis true.
- More information does not guarantee a better decision when its acquisition cost or the decision objective is ignored.
- Zero expected value of sample information under one loss does not imply that the observation has no scientific value under every other objective.
- A finite-budget stopping rule does not certify model-class completeness.
- A posterior preference is not, by itself, an empirical discrimination result when the likelihoods are identical.
- Decision-relevant equivalence is relative to the action set and loss function and can fail when either changes.

9. AUDIT CHECKLIST

Before applying this framework, record:
1. The candidate hypothesis set and its known omissions.
2. The observation distributions under each admissible experiment.
3. The exact experiment class and budget constraints.
4. The available actions and their loss function.
5. Any prior probabilities or robust uncertainty set and their justification.
6. Whether equivalence is exact or approximate.
7. Whether remaining uncertainty changes action rankings or only model beliefs.
8. Experiment costs and how they are compared with losses.
9. The decision-based or identification-based stopping criterion.
10. The tolerance required for any claimed target identification.
11. The consequences of model misspecification and omitted hypotheses.
12. The scope of the conclusion and the external assumptions on which it depends.

10. FINAL AUDIT CONCLUSION

A finite experiment program should distinguish three outcomes: (i) the hypotheses are empirically separated within the declared design; (ii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent but are decision-equivalent for the stated objective; or (iii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent while their loss profiles differ, requiring an explicit uncertainty-aware decision rule or a more discriminating experiment.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors: treating unresolved model identity as if it made every decision impossible, and treating a practical decision as if it proved which model is true.

The audit supplies a conditional decision framework. It does not establish a physical observation model, empirical result, unique optimal experiment, or universally valid stopping rule for OMEGA-ISOP.
