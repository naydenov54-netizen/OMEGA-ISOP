OMEGA-ISOP / M3.2
AUDIT #36
DECISION-RELEVANT OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE AND STOPPING RULES UNDER A FINITE EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
Date: 2026-10-29
Status: CONDITIONAL MATHEMATICAL AUDIT
Scope: decision relevance, observationally equivalent hypotheses, experiment selection, and stopping criteria.
Governance: this audit does not modify the Frozen Core, promote a claim to CE-V, or register an RL item.

0. PURPOSE AND RELATION TO PRIOR AUDITS

Audits #30–#33 examined inverse stability, experimental hypothesis separation, budget-constrained experiment design, and robust design under model uncertainty. Audits #34–#35 examined simplicity-based model selection and its formal limits.

This audit addresses a distinct decision question: when available experiments cannot distinguish candidate models, does that uncertainty necessarily prevent a defensible decision? Conversely, when should an experiment be selected because it can change a decision rather than merely increase a generic information score?

The conclusions below are conditional mathematical results. They do not establish that a particular OMEGA-ISOP physical model, observation map, loss function, or experimental family has already been specified.

1. FORMAL SETUP

Let H be a nonempty set of candidate hypotheses, E a declared set of admissible experiments, A a set of available actions, and D the observation space.

For each hypothesis h in H and experiment e in E, let P_h^e be the probability distribution of observations under that hypothesis and experiment. Let L(a,h) be the loss assigned to taking action a when h is the relevant state of the world.

The observation model, experiment class, action set, and loss function must be specified before a decision-theoretic conclusion can be evaluated. The results in Sections 3–4 use state-based loss L(a,h). If loss depends on the observation, use L(a,d,h) and define the relevant expected loss and posterior risk explicitly; equality of L(a,h) alone is not enough to establish decision equivalence for that observation-dependent objective.

2. EXACT OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE

Define h ~_E h' if and only if

P_h^e = P_{h'}^e for every e in E.

This is observational equivalence relative to the declared experiment class E. It is not a claim of metaphysical identity, universal equivalence under all possible experiments, or equivalence under an experiment excluded from E.

If h ~_E h', then no procedure using observations generated by experiments in E can distinguish h from h' through differences in their observation distributions alone. Any distinction made by a decision rule must therefore arise from prior information, additional assumptions, external evidence, or a different experiment class.

3. DECISION-RELEVANT EQUIVALENCE

Define decision-relevant equivalence by

h ~_L h' if and only if L(a,h) = L(a,h') for every a in A.

Proposition 1 (decision irrelevance within an observational class).
Suppose h ~_E h' and h ~_L h'. Then uncertainty between h and h' does not change the loss assigned to any available action. Resolving which of the two hypotheses is true is unnecessary for ranking actions by their losses, provided the stated action set and loss function are complete for the decision at hand.

Proof.
For every a in A, the equality L(a,h)=L(a,h') holds by definition of ~_L. Hence every pairwise action-loss comparison is unchanged when h is replaced by h'. The result concerns the specified decision objective only.

Boundary.
This proposition does not say that h and h' are scientifically equivalent in general. They may differ in predictions outside E, in unmodeled consequences, or under a different loss function or action set.

4. WHEN OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE MATTERS FOR A DECISION

If h ~_E h' but their loss profiles differ, available observations cannot distinguish the hypotheses through the declared experiments, even though the preferred action may depend on which hypothesis is true.

In that case, the decision cannot be justified by claiming that the data selected one hypothesis over the other. A decision can still be made using an explicit rule such as minimax loss, expected loss under stated prior probabilities, a robust constraint, or an external evidence source. These are decision rules under uncertainty, not empirical identification of the true hypothesis.

Proposition 2 (no data-only resolution within an exact equivalence class).
If h ~_E h', every observation procedure restricted to E has the same observation law under h and h'. Therefore, a test based only on those observations cannot have different sampling behavior under h and h' that would empirically identify which one holds.

This proposition does not prohibit a decision. It prohibits presenting that decision as if the permitted observations had discriminated the hypotheses.

5. EXPERIMENT VALUE MUST BE TIED TO THE DECISION TARGET

A generic information score and a decision-value score answer different questions. An experiment can provide information about parameters or hypotheses while leaving the ranking of available actions unchanged. Conversely, an experiment with modest total information can be valuable if it resolves an uncertainty that changes a high-consequence decision.

For a fully specified decision problem, one possible criterion is the expected reduction in Bayes risk. Let pi be an explicitly stated prior on H, and define the pre-experiment Bayes risk

R(pi) = min over a in A of sum over h in H of pi(h)L(a,h).

For experiment e, define its expected posterior Bayes risk as

R_e(pi) = E_{d under the prior-predictive distribution for e} [
  min over a in A of sum over h in H pi(h | d,e)L(a,h)
].

The expected value of sample information is

EVSI(e) = R(pi) - R_e(pi).

Under this loss-minimization convention, EVSI(e) is nonnegative for a well-defined decision problem when the decision-maker can ignore the observation and retain the original action. If experiment cost C(e) is included, a simple net-value criterion is EVSI(e) - C(e), provided the units and timing of cost and loss are made commensurable.

All of these quantities depend on the prior, likelihoods, action set, loss, experiment cost, and admissible designs. They are not defined by the Frozen Core unless those components are independently specified.

6. FINITE BUDGETS AND STOPPING

Let B be a finite experiment budget and let E_B be the set of experiment sequences admissible under that budget. A stopping rule should be stated against a declared objective, not chosen after observing which result is convenient.

A conditional decision-based stopping rule must compare the best remaining admissible continuation with stopping now, not merely evaluate each experiment in isolation.

For the current information state, define the value of a continuation policy as its expected terminal decision risk plus its remaining experiment costs, with all terms expressed in commensurable units. Stop when the minimum expected total value over all admissible continuation sequences or adaptive policies is no lower than the value of stopping now and choosing the best available action. Equivalently, stop when the maximum incremental expected reduction in decision risk minus incremental experiment costs is nonpositive, under the specified model set, prior or robust uncertainty rule, loss function, budget, and cost accounting.

If only one-step experiments are evaluated, the rule is myopic and is justified only when the design problem has conditions that make one-step evaluation sufficient. Complementary experiments can have positive joint value even when each experiment has nonpositive incremental value when considered alone. For a sequential design, the continuation value should account for future observations, choices, and costs, for example through a Bellman recursion when its assumptions apply.

This is a criterion within a specified decision model, not a universal stopping theorem. It may be invalid if the model set omits relevant hypotheses, the loss omits important consequences, the prior is unjustified, experiment costs are mismeasured, or the available continuation policies are incompletely enumerated.

A separate identification-based stopping rule may be appropriate when the target is a parameter or hypothesis rather than an action: stop only when the declared target is identified to the required tolerance over the feasible set, or explicitly report that the budget ended before that condition was met. Decision adequacy and scientific identification must not be conflated.

7. CONDITIONAL RESULTS

Result A.
Exact observational equivalence relative to E prevents data-only discrimination using experiments in E.

Result B.
If all actions have equal losses under two observationally equivalent hypotheses, uncertainty between those hypotheses is irrelevant to the ranking of those actions for that specified loss.

Result C.
If losses differ across observationally equivalent hypotheses, a decision may still be made by an explicit uncertainty-aware rule, but it cannot be described as data-based identification between those hypotheses.

Result D.
Expected value of sample information is decision-relative. Information that does not improve the best achievable expected decision loss may have zero decision value under the declared objective, even if it changes beliefs about the hypotheses.

Result E.
A stopping rule based on expected net decision value is conditional on the completeness of the model class, validity of probabilities or robust uncertainty sets, completeness of the loss, and consistent cost accounting.

8. NON-IMPLICATIONS

- Observational equivalence under E does not imply equivalence under every possible experiment.
- Failure to identify a hypothesis does not automatically make every decision impossible.
- A decision that is defensible under minimax loss or a stated prior does not prove the selected hypothesis true.
- More information does not guarantee a better decision when its acquisition cost or the decision objective is ignored.
- Zero expected value of sample information under one loss does not imply that the observation has no scientific value under every other objective.
- A finite-budget stopping rule does not certify model-class completeness.
- A posterior preference is not, by itself, an empirical discrimination result when the likelihoods are identical.
- Decision-relevant equivalence is relative to the action set and loss function and can fail when either changes.

9. AUDIT CHECKLIST

Before applying this framework, record:
1. The candidate hypothesis set and its known omissions.
2. The observation distributions under each admissible experiment.
3. The exact experiment class and budget constraints.
4. The available actions and their loss function.
5. Any prior probabilities or robust uncertainty set and their justification.
6. Whether equivalence is exact or approximate.
7. Whether remaining uncertainty changes action rankings or only model beliefs.
8. Experiment costs and how they are compared with losses.
9. The decision-based or identification-based stopping criterion.
10. The tolerance required for any claimed target identification.
11. The consequences of model misspecification and omitted hypotheses.
12. The scope of the conclusion and the external assumptions on which it depends.

10. FINAL AUDIT CONCLUSION

A finite experiment program should distinguish three outcomes: (i) the hypotheses are empirically separated within the declared design; (ii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent but are decision-equivalent for the stated objective; or (iii) the hypotheses remain observationally equivalent while their loss profiles differ, requiring an explicit uncertainty-aware decision rule or a more discriminating experiment.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors: treating unresolved model identity as if it made every decision impossible, and treating a practical decision as if it proved which model is true.

The audit supplies a conditional decision framework. It does not establish a physical observation model, empirical result, unique optimal experiment, or universally valid stopping rule for OMEGA-ISOP.

0. PURPOSE, SCOPE, AND READING RULE

This document provides a conditional decision-theoretic framework for distinguishing observational equivalence, decision-relevant equivalence, value of sample information, and stopping under a declared experimental design.

The claims are mathematical statements conditional on their assumptions. They do not establish that OMEGA-ISOP has a specified physical observation model, complete hypothesis class, justified prior, complete loss function, validated experiment-cost model, or empirical result.

Unless a section explicitly says otherwise:
• observations and policies are measurable;
• probabilities and expectations are well-defined;
• terminal loss is state-based, L(a,h), and does not directly depend on observed data;
• experiment costs are included in the objective when stated;
• all comparisons use the same model, feasible policy classes, budget constraints, loss convention, and remaining-cost convention;
• exact equivalence is considered only relative to the experiment or protocol class explicitly declared.

The robust/minimax discussion is separate from the operative expected-loss results. No expected-loss result should be transferred to a robust criterion without a separate proof.

1. FORMAL SETUP

Let (H, 𝓗) be a nonempty measurable hypothesis/state space, A a nonempty set of terminal actions, and E a declared set of admissible experiments. For each experiment e, let (D_e, 𝓓_e) be its observation space.

For each h in H and e in E, let P_h^e be the probability measure governing the observation from one non-repeated, non-adaptive performance of e under h.

Let L(a,h) be the terminal loss from action a when h is the relevant state. Unless expressly extended, L is state-based. If the actual objective uses L(a,d,h), path-dependent loss, or history-dependent utility, the relevant risks and value-of-information quantities must be defined for that objective separately; the state-based results below do not automatically apply.

A prior μ is a probability measure on (H, 𝓗). In discrete models, write μ(h) for the prior mass at h. All integrals, posterior distributions, and infima below require the relevant measurability and well-definedness assumptions stated in context.

A protocol class Π is a declared class of admissible experiment-selection and stopping protocols. A protocol may select experiments adaptively using observable history and may use internal randomization independent of h conditional on that history. For each h and π in Π, let Law_h^π denote the probability law of the complete observable history, including selected experiments and observations, and the stopping/termination record where relevant.

2. OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE

2.1. Single-experiment equivalence

For a specified experiment e, define

h ~_e h' iff P_h^e = P_{h'}^e

as probability measures on the same observation space (D_e, 𝓓_e).

2.2. Equivalence over a declared experiment set

Define

h ~_E h' iff P_h^e = P_{h'}^e for every e in E.

Thus ~_e concerns one specified experiment; ~_E concerns every experiment in the declared set E. Neither relation makes a claim about experiments outside E.

2.3. Protocol equivalence

Define

h ~_Π h' iff Law_h^π = Law_{h'}^π for every π in Π.

If Π includes every single non-adaptive performance of each e in E, then h ~_Π h' implies h ~_E h'. The converse does not generally hold.

Counterexample: let experiment e be performed twice. Under h, d₂ = d₁; under h', the two observations are independent. Each observation is Bernoulli(1/2) under both hypotheses, so the one-step marginal distributions are equal. But T = 1{d₁ = d₂} has probability 1 under h and 1/2 under h'. The full repeated-history laws differ. Equal marginal distributions therefore do not imply equal joint laws.

2.4. Conditional-kernel sufficient condition for protocol equivalence

Suppose that, conditional on h and the observable history, each next observation is governed by a kernel K_h(· | history,e), and the protocol chooses its next experiment according to a rule that depends only on the observable history and h-independent internal randomization. Equality of the relevant kernels under h and h' is sufficient for equality of full-history laws under a fixed protocol, subject to the following conditions:

(a) The initial observation kernel is equal under h and h'.

(b) Subsequent conditional kernels agree almost surely under the common history law, using regular conditional probabilities where needed. In continuous spaces, do not demand equality at every null history merely because it is mathematically describable.

(c) The equality condition covers every history and experiment choice that can be reached under the protocol being considered.

(d) To establish h ~_Π h', the condition must cover the union of histories and experiment choices reachable under every protocol in Π, not just those reached under one selected protocol.

(e) The kernels describe observable histories; any latent states are integrated out unless they are explicitly included in the observed state.

(f) Stopping and experiment selection are measurable functions or kernels of observable history and h-independent internal randomization.

(g) For infinite-horizon protocols, the trajectory law is well-defined from the kernels, for example by an Ionescu–Tulcea construction, and the stopping-time convention is stated.

For a fixed protocol with discrete observations, equality of conditional kernels on histories of positive probability is necessary as well as sufficient for equality of the corresponding history laws. In general measurable spaces, statements of necessity must be phrased up to the appropriate almost-sure equivalence of conditional distributions.

Counterexample to a protocol-specific reachability condition: suppose two hypotheses have equal kernels except after history d₁=1 when experiment e_b is selected. A protocol that never selects e_b after d₁=1 may produce equal laws under both hypotheses. Another admissible protocol that selects e_b there may produce different laws. Equality on histories reached by one protocol is not enough to establish equivalence for all protocols.

2.5. Interpretation boundary

Any distinction made by a test based on a declared protocol must come from a difference in the full observation laws, prior information, additional assumptions, external evidence, or a different experiment/protocol class. Observational equivalence is relative to the declared class, not metaphysical identity or universal scientific equivalence.

3. DECISION-RELEVANT EQUIVALENCE

Define

h ~_L h' iff L(a,h) = L(a,h') for every a in A.

This relation depends on the specified action set and loss function. It can fail if either changes.

3.1. Proposition 1: invariance of pre-data expected action loss

Discrete form. Let H be discrete, let μ be a prior probability mass function, and let h ≠ h' satisfy h ~_L h'. Let μ' be formed by moving an amount t of prior mass from h to h', with

μ'(h) = μ(h) - t,
μ'(h') = μ(h') + t,
0 ≤ t ≤ μ(h),

and μ' = μ elsewhere. Assume the expected loss of every action under μ and μ' is well-defined and finite. Then for every a in A,

E_{μ'}[L(a,H)] = E_μ[L(a,H)].

Consequently, the ranking of actions by pre-data expected loss and the pre-data Bayes risk

R(μ) = inf_{a∈A} E_μ[L(a,H)]

are unchanged.

Proof. The change in expected loss for any fixed a is

-t L(a,h) + t L(a,h') = 0,

because L(a,h)=L(a,h'). Thus each action's expected loss is unchanged, and so is the infimum over the same action set. QED.

Scope. This proposition concerns only pre-data expected action loss for state-based L and a fixed action set. It does not, by itself, establish invariance of posterior decisions, EVSI, or stopping values. Observational equivalence is not needed for this pre-data result.

General-measure caution. The phrase “move mass between two hypotheses” is used here in the discrete sense. In a general measurable space, changing a prior requires a separately specified measure transformation; the discrete proof must not be silently applied to continuous priors where individual points may have zero mass.

3.2. Counterexample: loss equivalence alone does not preserve EVSI

Let H={h₁,h₂,h₃}, A={a₁,a₂}, with losses

L(a₁,·)=(0,0,1),
L(a₂,·)=(1,1,0).

Thus h₁ ~_L h₂. Let one experiment have D={0,1}, with

P_{h₁}(d=1)=1,
P_{h₂}(d=1)=0,
P_{h₃}(d=1)=0.

Under prior μ_A=(1/2,0,1/2), the pre-data risk is R=1/2. The experiment reveals whether h₁ or h₃ holds, so post-data risk is R_e=0 and EVSI=1/2.

Under prior μ_B=(0,1/2,1/2), the observation is always d=0 and leaves h₂ and h₃ with equal posterior mass. Either action has expected loss 1/2, so R=1/2, R_e=1/2, and EVSI=0.

The total prior mass on {h₁,h₂} is 1/2 in both cases, and the pre-data action risks agree, but EVSI differs because h₁ and h₂ have different observation laws. This is why Proposition 1 cannot be extended to EVSI on loss equivalence alone.

3.3. Proposition 1b: conditions for invariance of experiment and policy values

Suppose h ~_L h' and h ~_Π h'. Assume:

(a) the same admissible policy class and feasibility rules apply under h and h';

(b) each relevant policy has the same expected remaining cost under h and h';

(c) terminal loss is state-based, measurable, and integrable enough for the expected objectives below to be well-defined;

(d) terminal actions are selected from the same action set using observable history and h-independent internal randomization;

(e) the objective is expected terminal loss plus expected remaining experiment costs.

For any admissible policy δ, let J_x(δ) be its expected terminal loss plus remaining cost under hypothesis x. Under the stated conditions, J_h(δ)=J_{h'}(δ). Therefore, for a discrete prior, moving mass between h and h' leaves the expected objective of every policy unchanged. It consequently leaves unchanged the infimum over any fixed policy class, including pre-data risk, post-experiment risk for a specified experiment, EVSI, V_stop and V_continue, whenever those values are defined using the same model and policy classes.

For the post-experiment risk R_e of a specified experiment e, the stated protocol-equivalence condition is sufficient only if the declared protocol class includes the single-run protocol that performs e (or if h ~_e h' is assumed directly). More generally, equality of full-history laws under a protocol class does not imply equality for an experiment that is outside that class. The same reasoning applies at histories where the two hypotheses have the same likelihood for that history and the relevant conditional objectives remain well-defined. Claims about posterior invariance must be restricted to histories with positive predictive probability, or stated almost surely under the predictive law.

Proof. For every fixed policy δ, equal full-history laws, equal terminal-loss profiles and equal expected costs imply J_h(δ)=J_{h'}(δ). Moving prior mass between hypotheses with equal J-values therefore leaves the prior-weighted value of each policy unchanged. Taking infima over the same policy class preserves equality. QED.

Limitations. If the hypotheses have different observation laws, different policy feasibility, different expected costs, or different terminal loss for some action, the result need not hold. For a robust criterion over a set of priors, an analogous invariance statement additionally requires the prior set and its updating convention to be compatible with the specified mass move. For a worst-case criterion over a fixed hypothesis set, a prior mass move has no direct effect because that criterion does not use the prior; equal policy values under h and h' are relevant to whether the two states contribute different worst-case values.

4. WHAT OBSERVATIONAL EQUIVALENCE DOES AND DOES NOT MEAN FOR DECISIONS

If h ~_Π h' but their loss profiles differ, the permitted observations cannot discriminate between them by differences in the full observation law, even though the preferred action may depend on which hypothesis is true.

A decision can still be made using an explicit rule, such as expected loss under a stated prior, minimax loss, a robust constraint, or external evidence. Such a decision is a choice under uncertainty; it is not empirical identification of the true hypothesis.

Proposition 2: no data-only resolution within an exact protocol-equivalence class. Let h ~_Π h', and let φ be a measurable test based on the observed history and internal randomization independent of h. Then φ has the same distribution under h and h'. In particular, its rejection probability is equal under the two hypotheses.

Proof. Since the full-history laws are equal and the test applies the same measurable rule and h-independent randomization, its induced distribution is equal under both hypotheses. QED.

In a dominated formulation where likelihoods are defined, equal full-history laws imply a likelihood ratio of 1 almost surely for the two hypotheses on the common support. For histories with positive probability under the relevant model, posterior odds between the two hypotheses therefore equal prior odds. This likelihood-ratio statement should not be used where the required domination or likelihood ratio is undefined.

This result does not prohibit a decision. It prohibits claiming that the permitted observations empirically selected one hypothesis over another when their full observation laws are identical.

5. VALUE OF SAMPLE INFORMATION

An information score and a decision-value score answer different questions. Information can change beliefs without changing the ranking of actions. An experiment can also have modest generic information value yet be decision-relevant if it changes a high-consequence action.

The following EVSI result applies only to state-based loss L(a,h).

5.1. Assumptions

(A1) μ is a proper prior probability measure on (H,𝓗).

(A2) For every a∈A, L(a,·) is measurable. Either L is bounded below by a finite constant and the risks are well-defined, or the relevant losses are integrable so that the expectations below are finite and well-defined.

(A3) For each experiment e, m_e is the prior-predictive distribution on D_e, and a regular conditional posterior μ(·|d,e) exists for m_e-almost every d. Standard Borel spaces are a sufficient common setting for such regular conditional distributions.

(A4) The posterior-risk infimum

d ↦ inf_{a∈A} ∫ L(a,h) μ(dh|d,e)

is measurable for purposes of integration. Sufficient conditions include countable A with measurable risks, or appropriate measurable-selection/standard-Borel conditions. If only universal measurability is available, expectations are taken under the completed predictive measure.

(A5) After observing d, the decision-maker may choose any action in the same set A.

(A6) R(μ) is finite, so EVSI is not defined by an indeterminate expression ∞−∞.

5.2. Definitions

R(μ) = inf_{a∈A} ∫_H L(a,h) μ(dh).

R_e(μ) = ∫_{D_e} [ inf_{a∈A} ∫_H L(a,h) μ(dh|d,e) ] m_e(dd).

EVSI(e) = R(μ) − R_e(μ).

For finite or countable H, the integrals reduce to sums where appropriate. Use a minimum only when attainment is established; otherwise use an infimum.

5.3. Proposition 3: nonnegativity of EVSI

Under (A1)–(A6), R_e(μ) ≤ R(μ), so EVSI(e) ≥ 0.

Proof. Fix any a₀∈A. For m_e-almost every d, the posterior infimum is no greater than the posterior risk of a₀. Integrating and applying the posterior disintegration identity gives

R_e(μ) ≤ ∫_{D_e}∫_H L(a₀,h) μ(dh|d,e)m_e(dd)
= ∫_H L(a₀,h) μ(dh).

Taking the infimum over a₀ yields R_e(μ)≤R(μ). The stated boundedness/integrability and finiteness conditions ensure the difference is well-defined. QED.

If the post-observation action set is smaller than A, if the risks are not well-defined, or if loss depends directly on observations, this proposition is not asserted. In particular, for L(a,d,h), EVSI must be defined against an explicit reference policy/objective and proved separately.

If every action has infinite expected loss, R and R_e may both be infinite, in which case EVSI as their difference is undefined.

If deterministic experiment cost C(e) is included in a one-step decision, EVSI(e)−C(e) is a net-value criterion only when loss and cost are commensurable and the cost timing/accounting is consistent. It is a one-step criterion, not a substitute for a full sequential policy comparison.

6. FINITE BUDGETS AND STOPPING

6.1. Scope and objective

Let B be a finite experimental budget and let the admissible experiment sequences/policies be defined by the budget and other stated constraints. The operative stopping comparison in this section is for the expected-loss criterion only.

At a given observable history, let δ be an admissible future policy that chooses subsequent experiments, a terminal time, and a terminal action, possibly using internal randomization independent of h. Define

J(δ) = expected terminal decision loss + expected remaining experiment costs,

where expectation is under the posterior/current uncertainty model at the current history. Costs already paid are sunk costs and are excluded. If costs are random or discounted, specify the expectation and discounting convention. Loss and costs must be in commensurable units.

Define:

D_stop = admissible policies that stop at the current history and choose a terminal action.

D_cont = admissible policies that take at least one further experiment before terminating.

Both classes must use the same loss function, uncertainty model, remaining-cost convention, budget constraints and other feasibility rules. A continuation policy includes future observations, later experiment choices, terminal action, and all remaining costs.

6.2. Termination and empty classes

Require every admissible policy to terminate almost surely after finitely many experiments. A finite budget alone does not guarantee this if experiment costs can approach zero. This termination requirement may be ensured by, for example, a finite upper bound on the number of experiments, a uniform lower bound c(e)≥c_min>0 on every experiment cost, or an explicit almost-sure finite-termination condition in the policy definition.

Use the convention inf ∅ = +∞. Define

V_stop = inf_{δ∈D_stop} J(δ),

V_continue = inf_{δ∈D_cont} J(δ).

If the admissible policy universe is partitioned into D_stop and D_cont, its global infimum is min(V_stop,V_continue). If no experiment is feasible, D_cont is empty and stopping is mandatory. If stopping is prohibited by the declared rules, D_stop is empty and V_stop=+∞.

6.3. Operational infimum rule

Compare the infimal values:

• If V_stop < V_continue, the stopping class has the lower infimum.
• If V_continue < V_stop, the continuation class has the lower infimum.
• If V_stop = V_continue, both classes have the same infimal value; tie-breaking may be set by convention, for example preferring to stop.

If V_continue < V_stop, the definition of infimum guarantees that there exists a feasible continuation policy δ with J(δ)<V_stop, even if V_continue is not attained. This proves that some continuation policy beats every stopping policy; it does not prove that an optimal continuation policy exists.

If V_stop < V_continue, the analogous statement holds: some stopping policy has value below V_continue, even if V_stop is not attained. Thus the comparison identifies the better policy class at the level of infimal values, but does not automatically provide a policy attaining the global infimum.

A simple operational convention is: choose the stopping class when V_stop≤V_continue, and the continuation class when V_continue<V_stop; then separately report whether the chosen class attains its infimum. This convention is a tie-break/decision convention, not a guarantee that an optimal policy exists.

6.4. Attainment and global optimality

• If V_stop < V_continue and V_stop is attained, an optimal stopping policy exists.
• If V_stop < V_continue and V_stop is not attained, no globally optimal policy exists: the global infimum is V_stop and no policy in the lower-valued class attains it.
• If V_continue < V_stop and V_continue is attained, an optimal continuation policy exists.
• If V_continue < V_stop and V_continue is not attained, no globally optimal policy exists.
• If V_stop = V_continue, attainment in either class yields a globally optimal policy in that class. If both are attained, both classes contain an optimal policy. If neither is attained, no global optimum exists.
• Whenever a class infimum is not attained, report the infimal value and non-attainment explicitly. An ε-optimal policy may exist by the definition of infimum when the class is nonempty and the infimum is finite, but it is not an optimal policy.

These statements assume the global admissible policy class is exactly the union of the two declared classes and the infima are well-defined.

6.5. Root versus sequential optimality

The comparison above concerns optimality at the current/root history. It does not automatically establish optimality at every possible future history, especially histories of probability zero under the current prior or selected policy. Sequential optimality requires appropriate Bellman/continuation conditions at the relevant histories and additional assumptions sufficient for the recursion.

6.6. Randomization

For the expected-loss criterion, a randomized mixture of policies has value equal to the average of the component values when the mixture is admissible and the objective is linear in the induced distribution. Therefore randomization cannot yield a value below the infimum of the component policy values. This statement does not carry over unchanged to worst-case criteria.

6.7. One-step versus multi-step evaluation

For deterministic cost C(e), the policy “perform e, then stop and choose the Bayes action” is a member of D_cont when feasible. Consequently,

V_continue ≤ inf_{e feasible} [R_e(μ) + C(e)]

when the notation and objective are aligned and R_e is the post-experiment terminal decision risk.

Therefore, the test EVSI(e)≤C(e) for every individual experiment only establishes that no evaluated one-step continuation beats the pre-experiment Bayes risk. It is not equivalent to the full multi-step stopping comparison. Complementary experiments may jointly be valuable even if each experiment alone has nonpositive net value.

Complementary-experiment example. Let θ=(x,y) be uniform on {(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)}. The available action a∈{0,1} has loss 0 if a=x XOR y and 1 otherwise. Initially R=1/2. Experiment e₁ reveals x and e₂ reveals y. Either experiment alone leaves x XOR y uniform, so each has EVSI 0. Together they identify x XOR y and reduce terminal risk to 0, giving joint EVSI 1/2. If each experiment costs c with 0<c<1/4, each one-step net value is −c, but the two-experiment plan has net value 1/2−2c>0.

6.8. Non-attainment example

Suppose after n experiments the terminal risk is r_n=2^{-n}, with r₀=1, and experiment n costs ε_n=4^{-n}. The total cost of any finite sequence is within the finite budget B=∑_{n≥1}4^{-n}=1/3. The value after n experiments is

r_n + ∑_{k=1}^n 4^{-k},

and it decreases strictly toward 1/3 without reaching 1/3 at any finite n. Over policies required to stop after finitely many experiments, the continuation infimum is not attained. A finite budget does not by itself ensure attainment.

6.9. Limitations of the stopping result

The stopping comparison is valid only if the objective, model, feasible policy classes, budget, loss function and cost accounting are consistently specified. It can fail to answer the intended real-world question if the model omits relevant hypotheses, the prior is unjustified, the loss omits important consequences, the observation process is misspecified, costs are mismeasured, or the admissible policy class is incomplete.

A separate identification-based stopping rule may be appropriate when the target is a parameter or hypothesis rather than an action. Such a rule must specify the target, tolerance, uncertainty criterion and feasible set. If the budget is exhausted before the criterion is met, report that identification was not achieved. Decision adequacy and scientific identification are different objectives.

6.10. Robust/minimax criterion — separate, conditional formulation

A robust criterion is not interchangeable with the expected-loss criterion. One possible static robust value is

V_x^rob = inf_{δ∈D_x} sup_{Q∈Γ} E_Q[L_terminal + C_remaining],
x ∈ {stop, continue},

where the uncertainty set Γ, policy class D_x, terminal-loss convention, and treatment of costs are explicitly stated. The supremum is over the total loss-plus-cost objective, not automatically the sum of separate suprema. For example, if two states have (loss,cost) pairs (1,0) and (0,1), the supremum of loss plus cost is 1, while the sum of the separate suprema is 2.

Γ must be identified as a set of states/hypotheses for a frequentist minimax criterion or as a set of probability measures for a Γ-minimax criterion. Minimax loss and minimax regret are different objectives. The policy class must state whether randomization is allowed: with losses L(a₁,·)=(0,1) and L(a₂,·)=(1,0), each pure action has worst-case loss 1, whereas the 50/50 randomized mixture has worst-case expected loss 1/2.

If uncertainty sets are updated after observations, the update rule must be specified. Rectangularity or a suitable time-consistency condition is required for many dynamic robust-programming/Bellman recursions, but is not a universal prerequisite for merely defining a static robust objective. Without suitable conditions, an ex-ante robust value and a sequentially re-optimized value can differ.

Example of dynamic inconsistency without rectangularity (for a specified pure-action class). Let the state be (x,y)∈{0,1}² and Γ={P₁,P₂}, where P₁ is uniform on {(0,0),(1,1)} and P₂ is uniform on {(0,1),(1,0)}. Thus x is uniform under both measures, while y=x under P₁ and y=1−x under P₂. Observe x and then guess y, with 0–1 loss. Restrict terminal guesses to deterministic actions and do not allow randomization, both ex ante and after observing x.

An ex-ante policy is a deterministic function f:{0,1}→{0,1}. The constant policies f(x)=0 and f(x)=1 each have error probability 1/2 under both P₁ and P₂. The policies f(x)=x and f(x)=1−x have worst-case error 1. Therefore the ex-ante minimax value over all deterministic policies is 1/2. After either observed value x, however, the conditional distributions retained from P₁ and P₂ assign y to opposite values. Either deterministic guess then has worst-case conditional error 1. Re-optimizing conditionally thus yields value 1 at each history, although the ex-ante value is 1/2. This is a genuine dynamic inconsistency for this non-rectangular uncertainty set and this pure-action class.

The example depends on excluding randomized guesses. If a 50/50 randomized guess is admissible, its error is 1/2 under each conditional model too, so this particular discrepancy disappears. The example therefore illustrates why the action/randomization class and the uncertainty-set update rule must be stated; it is not a universal prescription for every robust updating scheme.
For robust values, do not automatically assume

V^rob = min(V_stop^rob,V_continue^rob)

unless the policy classes and the robust objective make that identity valid. In general, infimum and supremum operations over policy classes and uncertainty sets require careful ordering and justification.

7. CONDITIONAL RESULTS

Result A. If h ~_Π h', no measurable test based only on the full observable history of any admissible protocol in Π has a different distribution under h and h'.

Result B. If h ~_L h', moving prior mass between h and h' in the discrete setup of Proposition 1 leaves the pre-data expected loss of every action unchanged. It does not generally preserve EVSI or stopping values. Such invariance requires the additional conditions in Proposition 1b, including protocol equivalence, equal expected policy costs, common policy feasibility, and a well-defined expected-loss objective.

Result C. If protocol-equivalent hypotheses have different loss profiles, a decision may still be made using an explicit uncertainty-aware rule, but it must not be described as data-based identification between those hypotheses.

Result D. Under assumptions (A1)–(A6), EVSI is nonnegative. If an experiment leaves the infimum of expected decision loss unchanged, its EVSI is zero under the specified loss and model, even if it changes beliefs about hypotheses.

Result E. The stopping comparison is conditional on a sufficiently specified hypothesis/model class, loss function, observation process, policy class, termination condition, and consistent cost accounting. The expected-loss operational comparison uses V_stop and V_continue as defined in Section 6. A robust formulation must separately specify the robust objective, uncertainty set, policy/randomization class and update rule. Rectangularity or an appropriate time-consistency condition is required when the chosen robust analysis relies on dynamic programming/Bellman recursion, not merely to define every static robust objective.

8. NON-IMPLICATIONS

• Observational equivalence under E or Π does not imply equivalence under every possible experiment or protocol.
• Equal single-experiment marginals do not imply equal joint laws for repeated or dependent observations.
• Failure to identify a hypothesis does not automatically make every decision impossible.
• A decision defensible under a stated prior or minimax criterion does not prove the selected hypothesis true.
• More information does not guarantee a better net decision when its acquisition cost or the objective is ignored.
• Zero EVSI under one loss does not imply that an observation has no scientific value under every other objective.
• A finite budget does not, by itself, exclude infinitely many experiments or guarantee attainment of an optimum.
• A posterior preference is not empirical discrimination when the full observation laws are identical.
• Decision-relevant equivalence depends on the action set and loss function.
• Loss equivalence alone does not imply invariance of EVSI or stopping values.
• A one-step EVSI-versus-cost test is not equivalent to a multi-step stopping rule.
• Exact-equivalence results do not establish approximate equivalence or weak distinguishability; those require quantitative definitions and separate bounds.
• A static robust criterion does not automatically justify a dynamically consistent Bellman recursion.

10. FINAL AUDIT CONCLUSION

A finite experiment program should distinguish at least four outcomes:

(i) hypotheses are empirically separated within the declared design;

(ii) hypotheses remain observationally equivalent but are decision-equivalent under the stated action set and loss;

(iii) hypotheses remain observationally equivalent while their loss profiles differ, requiring an explicit uncertainty-aware decision rule, external evidence, or a more discriminating experiment; or

(iv) hypotheses are not exactly equivalent but are distinguishable only below a specified tolerance.

Case (iv) is outside the exact-equivalence results here and requires a quantitative definition of approximate equivalence and its own bounds.

This framework prevents two opposite errors: treating unresolved model identity as making every decision impossible, and treating a practical decision as proof of which model is true.

The proposed revision gives conditional results for single-experiment and protocol-level observational equivalence, decision-relevant loss equivalence, expected value of sample information, and stopping under a declared expected-loss objective. It explicitly separates the static robust criterion from dynamic robust recursion. It does not establish a physical observation model, empirical result, unique optimal experiment, complete model class, or universally valid stopping rule for OMEGA-ISOP.
